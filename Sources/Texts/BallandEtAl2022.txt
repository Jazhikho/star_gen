## Abstract
Economic complexity offers a potentially powerful paradigm to understand key societal
issues and challenges of our time. The underlying idea is that growth, development,
technological change, income inequality, spatial disparities, and resilience are the visible
outcomes of hidden systemic interactions. The study of economic complexity seeks to
understand the structure of these interactions and how they shape various socioeconomic
processes. This emerging field relies heavily on big data and machine learning techniques.
This brief introduction to economic complexity has three aims: (1) to summarize key
theoretical foundations and principles of economic complexity; (2) to briefly review the
tools and metrics developed in the economic complexity literature that exploit information
encoded in the structure of the economy to find new empirical patterns; (3) to highlight
insights from economic complexity to improve prediction and political decision-making.
Institutions including the World Bank, the European Commission, the World Economic Forum,
the OECD, and a range of national and regional organizations have embraced the principles
of economic complexity and its analytical framework.

## Major claims, findings, models, assumptions, and limitations
- **Economic complexity index (ECI):** A nation's ECI captures the diversity AND
  sophistication (ubiquity-adjusted) of its exports. High-ECI countries export many
  diverse, low-ubiquity (rare) products. ECI predicts future economic growth better than
  GDP per capita or raw export diversity alone.
- **Product Space:** Products cluster into a network where proximity = probability of
  comparative-advantage co-occurrence. Countries can only move into nearby products.
  This means technological capability grows in path-dependent steps.
- **Complexity Fitness Index (CFI):** An alternative to ECI (Tacchella et al. 2012)
  that uses nonlinear iteration; better suited for developing economies where ECI
  underperforms.
- **Key empirical patterns:**
  - ECI strongly predicts 5-10 year GDP per capita growth (r² ~ 0.5–0.7 in cross-country
    regressions) with 5–15 year lead time.
  - Diversification is easier to achieve via product-space proximity moves than via
    development assistance or institutional reform alone.
  - Income inequality within countries is negatively correlated with ECI; more complex
    economies are more equal on average (counterintuitive: same level as Arvidsson's
    finding at the within-city scale).
  - Regional disparities within countries track regional ECI differences.
- **Policy implications:** Countries/regions should invest in capabilities adjacent to
  their current product space position, not jump to unrelated sectors.
- **Limitation:** ECI is export-based and misses domestic service complexity; services
  (banking, software, biotech) are undercounted.
- **Limitation:** Product space proximity is historically inferred from co-occurrence;
  may not transfer to radically new industries (AI, synthetic biology).
- **Limitation:** Short-term political shocks can decouple ECI from growth predictions.

## StarGen applicability
Provides the **product-space / path-dependency framework** for modeling technological
capability development in a civilization. Complements Bettencourt (population → output
scaling) and Arvidsson (output → inequality) with a structural model of what a civilization
CAN produce given its current capability base.

In StarGen's civilization generator:
- `TechLevel` is not just population-dependent — it depends on the civilization's
  accumulated capability diversity (analogous to ECI)
- A civilization at TL 8 cannot jump directly to TL 12 without intermediate steps
- Trade codes are path-dependent: a world exports what it has been able to develop
  from its starting conditions (planetary resources × history × contact with neighbors)
- Economic complexity provides a principled reason why isolated worlds fall behind
  connected trade-network worlds even at the same population

## Parameters, constraints, and mechanics implications

**Economic Complexity Index → Tech Level mapping:**
```
// ECI ranges empirically from ~ -3.5 (least complex) to +3.5 (most complex)
// Normalized ECI (0-1 scale) → maps approximately to TL fraction
// TL 0-4 (pre-industrial):   ECI_norm ~ 0.0–0.25
// TL 5-8 (industrial):        ECI_norm ~ 0.25–0.60
// TL 9-12 (advanced):         ECI_norm ~ 0.60–0.85
// TL 13+ (interstellar):      ECI_norm ~ 0.85–1.0
// Note: ECI alone doesn't capture the energy technology needed for TL 9+;
// supplement with a separate energy-sector capability index.
```

**Product-space path-dependency:**
```csharp
// A civilization's reachable tech level is bounded by adjacency to current capabilities.
// Each "tech step" requires capability prerequisites to be met.
// Simple model: TechLevel can advance by at most DeltaTL per generation of contact.
// DeltaTL = base_rate * connectivity_bonus * ECI_growth_rate
// base_rate ~ 0.5 TL per 100 years (isolated)
// connectivity_bonus ~ 2-5x for worlds in active trade networks
// DeltaTL cap: ~2 per 100 years even with maximum connectivity (no quantum jumps)
double reachableTL = currentTL + deltaPerGeneration * Math.Min(connectivityIndex, 5.0);
```

**Product space → Trade codes:**
- Worlds early in ECI development export raw resources (agricultural, mining trade codes)
- Mid-ECI worlds develop manufacturing and specialization
- High-ECI worlds export diverse high-value goods and services (industrial, high-tech codes)
```
ECI_norm | Likely Trade Codes
0.0-0.2  | Ag, Na, Ni (agricultural, non-agricultural, non-industrial)
0.2-0.4  | In, Mi, Re (industrial, mining, refinery)
0.4-0.6  | Ht, Ri (high tech emerging, rich)
0.6-1.0  | Ht, Ri, Ph, Di (high tech, rich, pharmaceutical, diverse)
```

**Connectivity bonus (trade route access):**
- Isolated world: connectivity_index = 0 → slow tech development
- One trade partner: connectivity_index = 1 → moderate growth
- Active starport (class A/B): connectivity_index = 3–5 → rapid capability growth
This directly motivates why Starport class matters for technological development pace.

## StarGen mechanics translation

### Economic complexity → Civilization capability model
BallandEtAl2022 provides the structural foundation for StarGen's economic-capability
model: what a civilization can produce, trade, and develop depends on its position in
the product space, not just its population.

**Civilization ECI profile generation:**
```csharp
// Starting ECI is set by:
// 1. Planetary resource endowment (affects initial product space entry points)
// 2. Contact history (trade routes → connectivity boosts)
// 3. Age of civilization (more time → more ECI growth steps)
double startingECI = PlanetaryResourceECI(planet)
                   + ConnectivityBonus(tradeRoutes)
                   + AgeFactor(civilizationAgeYears);
startingECI = Math.Clamp(startingECI, -3.5, 3.5);

// Map to TechLevel
int techLevel = ECIToTechLevel(startingECI);

// Map to Trade codes (primary export basket)
List<TradeCode> tradeCodes = ECIToTradeCodes(startingECI, planet.ResourceProfile);
```

**Product-space path dependency rule:**
No civilization can be at TechLevel 12 without passing through TL 5-9 intermediate
stages UNLESS it received deliberate technology transfer from an advanced neighbor.
Implement this as a constraint on the maximum achievable TL given civilization age
and connectivity history.

**User lever:** `EconomicComplexityModel` (enum: ProductSpace / SimpleScaling)
- ProductSpace: use ECI path-dependency model (BallandEtAl2022 + RenEtAl2024)
- SimpleScaling: use population-only Bettencourt scaling (simpler, less realistic)
