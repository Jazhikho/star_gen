## Abstract
Recent James Webb Space Telescope observations of cool, rocky exoplanets reveal a probable
lack of thick atmospheres, suggesting prevalent escape of the 'secondary' atmospheres formed
after losing primordial hydrogen. Yet, simulations indicate that hydrodynamic escape of
secondary atmospheres, composed of nitrogen and carbon dioxide, requires intense fluxes of
ionizing radiation (XUV) to overcome the effects of high molecular weight and efficient line
cooling. This transonic outflow of hot, ionized metals (not hydrogen) presents a novel
astrophysical regime ripe for exploration. We introduce an analytic framework to determine
which planets retain or lose their atmospheres, positioning them on either side of the cosmic
shoreline. We model the radial structure of escaping atmospheres as polytropic expansions —
power-law relationships between density and temperature driven by local XUV heating. Our
approach diagnoses line cooling with a three-level atom model and incorporates how
ion-electron interactions reduce mean molecular weight. Crucially, hydrodynamic escape onsets
for a threshold XUV flux dependent upon the atmosphere's gravitational binding. Ensuing
escape rates either scale linearly with XUV flux when weakly ionized (energy-limited) or are
controlled by a collisional-radiative thermostat when strongly ionized. Thus, airlessness is
determined by whether the XUV flux surpasses the critical threshold during the star's active
periods, accounting for expendable primordial hydrogen and revival by volcanism. Applications
include young Sun Mars and Earth, LHS 1140 b and c, and TRAPPIST-1 b.

## Major claims, findings, models, assumptions, and limitations
- **Secondary atmosphere escape is the new frontier:** JWST results (as of 2025-2026) show
  many cool rocky exoplanets lack thick atmospheres. The question is no longer only "did the
  primordial H/He envelope escape?" but "did the secondary (volcanic/outgassed) N₂-CO₂
  atmosphere also escape?"
- **Cosmic shoreline applies to secondary atmospheres too:** The Zahnle & Catling "cosmic
  shoreline" (escape velocity vs. insolation) originally demarcated primordial H/He
  retention. This paper extends the concept to heavy secondary atmospheres (N₂, CO₂).
- **Novel escape regime:** Heavy molecules (mean molecular weight ~28-44 g/mol) are much
  harder to escape than hydrogen (2 g/mol). They require transonic outflow of hot ionized
  metals at XUV fluxes well above the primordial hydrogen threshold.
- **Two escape regimes:**
  - Energy-limited: escape rate scales linearly with XUV flux (weakly ionized atmosphere)
  - Collisional-radiative thermostat: escape rate saturates (strongly ionized; more efficient
    cooling limits further acceleration)
- **XUV threshold for secondary atmosphere loss:** There is a critical XUV flux above which
  hydrodynamic escape of secondary N₂/CO₂ atmosphere occurs. Below this threshold,
  the atmosphere is retained. (Specific numeric value: verify against PDF — depends on
  planet mass and gravity.)
- **Volcanism revival:** Even if an atmosphere is stripped, volcanic outgassing can replenish
  it. The paper accounts for this "revival" mechanism. Net atmospheric fate depends on
  the balance between XUV loss rate and volcanic supply rate.
- **Application: LHS 1140 b/c:** LHS 1140 b (inner, higher XUV) may be on the edge;
  LHS 1140 c appears to be in the retained regime.
- **Application: TRAPPIST-1 b:** Modeling suggests TRAPPIST-1 b may be airless or
  have lost its secondary atmosphere due to high XUV flux, consistent with JWST
  measurements showing no thermal emission signature of a thick atmosphere.
- **Application: Young Mars/Earth:** The model recovers the expected outcome —
  Mars lost its secondary atmosphere; Earth retained it — providing validation.
- **Limitation:** Analytic polytropic model; full hydrodynamics may produce different
  results for complex atmospheres with multiple species.
- **Limitation:** Volcanism rate is poorly constrained for exoplanets; revival probability
  is speculative.
- **Limitation:** Requires XUV flux history as input; XUV evolution model (Ribas2015,
  LugerBarnes2015) needed to apply.

**Two-step atmospheric retention model (LugerBarnes + Chatterjee et al.):**

Step 1: Primordial H/He envelope             Step 2: Secondary atmosphere
LugerBarnes2015 + Ribas2015 (XUV history) → ChatterjeeEtAl2026 (N2/CO2 retention)

**Cosmic shoreline for secondary atmospheres:**
```csharp
// Secondary atmosphere retention check (Chatterjee et al. 2026)
// Based on: XUV flux at peak active period vs. escape threshold
double xuvPeakFlux = star.XUVPeakFluxAtPlanet(planet);  // from Ribas2015 model
double escapeThresholdXUV = ComputeSecondaryEscapeThreshold(
    planet.MassEarth, planet.RadiusEarth, planet.SurfaceGravity);
// Threshold scales with gravitational binding energy

bool secondaryAtmosphereStripped = xuvPeakFlux > escapeThresholdXUV;

if (secondaryAtmosphereStripped)
{
    // Check volcanism revival
    bool tectonicallyActive = planet.TectonicRegime != TectonicRegime.StagnantLid;
    double p_revival = tectonicallyActive ? 0.40 : 0.10;  // active volcanism needed
    if (rng.NextDouble() < p_revival)
    {
        planet.AtmosphereType = AtmosphereType.Thin_CO2;   // volcanic revival
        planet.AtmospherePressureBar = 0.01 + rng.NextDouble() * 0.5;
    }
    else
    {
        planet.AtmosphereType = AtmosphereType.Airless;    // fully stripped
        planet.AtmospherePressureBar = 0.0;
    }
}
```

**M-dwarf emphasis:**
For late M-dwarfs (M4+), extended XUV active period (LugerBarnes2015: up to 3 Gyr for
M8V) means secondary atmospheres face a much longer stripping window. Combined with
low volcanism probability (stagnant-lid common for low-mass rocky planets), airlessness
is a likely outcome for M-dwarf HZ planets:
```csharp
// M-dwarf secondary atmosphere fate (combined Chatterjee + LugerBarnes + Ribas)
if (star.SpectralType == SpectralType.M && star.TemperatureK < 3500)
{
    // Extended XUV phase: secondary atmosphere very vulnerable
    double p_retainSecondary = 0.15;  // rough estimate; verify numeric value against PDF
    planet.HasSecondaryAtmosphere = rng.NextDouble() < p_retainSecondary;
}
```
